ABOUT THE STUDIO

Games that shouldn't work... but somehow do.

One Guy. Too Many Ideas.

A lifelong gamer. A pile of ideas. Finally, a way to make them.

Single dad. Full-time tech. Game dev in my spare time, with a passion for perfection and detail.

I don't code the traditional way. I direct, refine, and obsessively iterate with AI tools until the game in my head becomes the one on the screen. Pixel art RPGs, interactive stories, and whatever else refuses to leave me alone. One person. No team. No shortcuts on the feel.

How I Got Here

I've been playing games since the '80s, from Mario to PC gaming and point-and-click adventures like King's Quest. I've always wanted to make things of my own. Getting the ideas out of my head was the difficult part.

I can't code or draw from scratch, and I don't have the budget to hire a programmer, artist, composer and everyone else a game normally needs. But I know my way around Photoshop, and I've spent years editing gameplay videos, GTA videos and audio: finding the timing, making the cuts, putting little stories and jokes together.

For a long time, video was the closest I could get to making my own worlds. Around 2022–2023, I started experimenting with AI tools, and suddenly there was a possibility that hadn't existed for me before. Maybe I could actually make some of these things.

First, Several Hundred Creepy Dolls

One of my first serious experiments was a card game about strange, damaged, forgotten dolls. Hundreds of them, each with abilities and backstories. A doll abandoned outside for decades should play differently from a porcelain doll that spent its life untouched on a shelf.

That taught me how much work there still was: regenerate the image, edit it, name the doll, write its history, then figure out whether its abilities work with everything else. The project got too big and went on the shelf. Appropriately enough for a doll game. I'd still like to return to it.

Copy. Paste. Break Something. Repeat.

Next came a blockchain game and a lot of fighting the tools. I'd ask ChatGPT for a change, paste the code into the project, run it, copy the error back and repeat. And repeat. Eventually it would start forgetting how the game worked. That project went nowhere, and I'm probably leaving it there. The lessons came with me.

A Personality Test Got Out of Hand

The Department of Questionable Intent started as a weird personality test. Naturally, I couldn't leave it alone. Soon there were clearance levels, offices to explore, things to unlock and a fictional organization with much more going on beneath the surface. My ambition got ahead of the tools again, so it went on hold. Not abandoned. Just waiting.

One piece of that world became WRFM — Workplace Relief Frequency: a fictional radio station with music, a host, call-ins, developer jokes and commercials for businesses that sound almost legitimate until you listen to what they're selling.

WRFM was also where I discovered Claude Code. Having AI work directly with the project files instead of constantly copying code back and forth was huge. Now the station works. You can actually listen to it. A few years earlier, I wouldn't have imagined building that myself.

Flow Programming

A new stage in development.

Most people call it vibe coding. I call it  Flow Programming.

Flow Programming is the enhanced, disciplined version of working with AI — not just throwing prompts and hoping for the best. It’s a continuous current of direction, refinement, and obsession. I stay inside the idea, push back on the machine, rebuild what doesn’t feel right, and keep iterating until the game stops feeling like a prototype and starts feeling like the thing that lived in my head.

The game runs. You can play it. Systems work. But nothing is locked. Balance, design, features, and feel remain fluid while the real work happens — the quiet, stubborn fine-tuning that turns “it works” into “this is it.”

One person. No traditional code. No team. Just  Flow Programming.

Why "Brok3n by Design"?

Look, "Flawless by Design" felt like a lie, and the domain was probably taken anyway.

The honest version: everything here starts as a glorious mess — half-finished systems, features held together with tape and stubbornness, and at least one bug I've officially reclassified as "a mechanic." Then I iterate until the broken parts either become the best part or quietly vanish before anyone notices. The cracks aren't a flaw in the plan. They are the plan.

And the "3" instead of an "e"? Every indie dev is contractually required to swap at least one vowel for a number. I don't make the rules. I just quietly resent them.

What I Believe

No Shortcuts on the Feel

A game can be rough around the edges, but it has to feel right. I'll rebuild the same system ten times until it plays the way it does in my head.

Flow Programming

I don't write traditional code — I direct and wrestle AI tools the way a designer wrestles systems, iterating hard and cutting whatever doesn't earn its place.

Strange Beats Safe

I'd rather make something memorable and a little broken than something polished and forgettable.

One Person, All In

No team, no committee, no "good enough." Every pixel, sound, and awkward joke is a deliberate choice by one stubborn human.

Then the Games Started Becoming Real

Pocket Wilds became a retro adventure with Zelda influences, exploration and bigger battles that switch to turn-based combat. I put together the art, music, sound, dialogue and a trailer using AI tools and my own editing. It still needs testing and polish, but it's playable. An idea in my head became a real game.

What If Your Character Was Basically One Pixel?

That became Speck. Zoom in and that tiny speck becomes a little grid-based character. One of my favourite ideas is Speck Studio, an in-game pixel editor for customizing characters, furniture, buildings and objects. Underneath that deliberately simple look is a whole world of survival, crafting, building and exploration. Apparently even my smallest idea had to become enormous.

The One I Keep Wanting to Play

I love dice games, card games and how something like Balatro builds so much around a simple mechanic. Glass & Fortune grew out of that, and this one surprised me. It's close to finished, the game works, and I can finish a run and want another. I actually like playing it. That's when this whole experiment started feeling real.

It's planned to be my first commercial Steam release. That's exciting and slightly terrifying. There are screenshots, trailers, testing and packaging to finish, and eventually I'll have to put it in front of actual players. Yes, I'm nervous about reviews. Some criticism will be fair, some people won't like the game, and some people on the internet just enjoy setting things on fire. I'm probably going to need a thicker skin. But I'm still going to release it.

And Apparently I Can't Stop Having Ideas

I've always liked storage-auction shows, which led to Bid & Buried. I wanted more than buying a locker, selling everything and watching a number go up. I wanted objects with histories, strange discoveries, collections and rival bidders with personalities. You're hunting for interesting things as much as expensive ones. There's still a lot of artwork and audio to create, but the thing I pictured is starting to exist on screen. That still feels kind of crazy.

There are more ideas waiting: a huge maze where choices have consequences, even a comedy/skit project. A shortage of ideas has never been the problem. Figuring out how to make them exist was.

So, About AI...

AI didn't replace my development team. It gave me the ability to have one.

I understand why artists and programmers are worried. There are real questions about training data, copyright, disclosure and creative jobs. I don't want a future where companies with the money to hire creative people fire them because generating everything is cheaper.

My situation is a guy at home with a full-time job and a pile of ideas. There was never a development team or a budget to hire one. Without these tools, most of these projects would have stayed in my head.

I still decide what the games are. I edit artwork, work with sound, rewrite ideas, change mechanics and play things until I understand what's wrong. I decide what fits the world, what's funny and what needs to go. Sometimes I spend hours on something that ends up deleted.

Sometimes AI gives me an idea better than mine. Sometimes it gives me absolute garbage. That's part of the process too.

I don't really think of myself as an artist or a programmer. I'm somebody who likes making things. For most of my life, there was a pretty big wall between having an idea for a game and actually making one. That wall has gotten a lot smaller. So now I'm making them.
